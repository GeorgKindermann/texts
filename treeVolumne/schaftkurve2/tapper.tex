    \begin{abstract}
      Accurate estimation of stem taper and volume is fundamental to forest management, biomass assessment, and timber valuation. This study presents an analytical stem taper model with continuous diameter and slope transitions ($C^1$ continuity) for Norway spruce (\textit{Picea abies}), based on a dataset of \num{15684} sampled stems from Switzerland. The model employs a composite power-function formulation partitioned at a morphologically defined inflection point, allowing basal root flare and upper stem form to be represented separately within a biologically consistent framework applicable across tree sizes.

These models predict shape parameters as functions of total tree height and a reference diameter, thereby avoiding strict dependence on diameter at breast height. The resulting formulation yields fully analytical expressions for stem diameter, height inversion, and volume integration, eliminating the need for numerical integration in operational use.

      The inflection point increases with tree height at a diminishing rate, reflecting systematic ontogenetic changes in stem architecture, whilst the shape exponents capture the transition from conical juvenile forms to more parabolic stem profiles in mature trees. Incorporating an additional upper-stem diameter measurement at $0.65H$ reduces this to $\sigma = 0.0424$, while a fully tree-specific three-point calibration further reduces it to $\sigma = 0.0279$. Sensitivity analyses indicate that measurement configuration strongly governs error propagation, with higher-positioned upper-stem anchors providing improved robustness under realistic field noise.

      Overall, the proposed framework provides a unified analytical solution for stem reconstruction that combines computational efficiency with biological interpretability. By generalising beyond fixed breast-height assumptions and allowing flexible multi-point calibration, it establishes a consistent basis for stem form and volume estimation from young stands to mature timber in large-scale forest inventory systems.

\section{Introduction}

Tree stem form reflects the cumulative effects of growth, environmental conditions, and mechanical adaptation throughout a tree's life. In practical forest inventories, however, this rich structural information is typically reduced to two measurements: diameter at breast height (DBH, measured at \qty{1.3}{m} above ground) and total tree height. These simple metrics provide a pragmatic basis for estimating stem volume, biomass, and timber assortments, and they remain the standard in operational forestry. Yet, despite their widespread use, reliance on DBH and height alone conceals the continuous variation in stem taper that governs the vertical distribution of woody biomass.

Accurate representation of stem form from such limited inputs remains a central challenge in forest mensuration. Beyond total volume estimation, a robust model must be capable of predicting diameters at arbitrary heights and enabling the computation of merchantable timber assortments between any two points along the stem. Achieving this level of reconstruction from standard inventory variables requires a formulation that is both mathematically well-defined and biologically consistent, bridging the gap between operational simplicity and detailed stem geometry.

A wide range of modelling approaches has been proposed, including form-factor methods, variable-exponent taper equations, segmented polynomial functions, and spline-based representations. While these approaches have been successfully applied in different contexts, they often involve trade-offs between biological interpretability, computational efficiency, measurement requirements, and analytical tractability. In particular, many formulations either rely on numerical integration for volume estimation or require dense sectional measurements to accurately capture individual-tree variability. Moreover, DBH-based formulations may be sensitive to basal swellings, buttresses, or root flare, meaning that apparent differences in taper may partly reflect measurement artefacts rather than true geometric variation.

Stem form is also strongly influenced by ontogenetic development and environmental conditions. Young trees typically exhibit near-conical stems, whereas mature individuals develop more differentiated profiles characterised by pronounced basal curvature and increasingly cylindrical upper sections. Stand density, competitive interactions, and mechanical loading further modulate stem taper, leading to substantial variability even within homogeneous stands.

Recent advances in forest inventory methods increasingly provide access to additional stem diameter information through sectional sampling, terrestrial laser scanning, photogrammetry, and mobile sensing systems. While these developments create new opportunities for improving taper models, they also introduce the challenge of integrating multi-point measurements without sacrificing analytical simplicity, computational efficiency, or compatibility with traditional inventory frameworks.

This study addresses this question by introducing a continuous analytical stem taper model for Norway spruce (\textit{Picea abies}) that operates primarily from DBH and total height, while allowing the integration of up to two additional diameter measurements when available. The model is based on a composite power-function formulation that separates basal root flare from the upper stem through a dynamically estimated inflection point, ensuring a biologically meaningful transition between stem segments. The resulting profile is $C^1$-continuous, guaranteeing smooth variation in both diameter and taper along the entire stem.

A key feature of the framework is its fully analytical structure, which yields closed-form solutions for stem diameter, height inversion, and volume integration without the need for numerical integration. This enables efficient application in large-scale inventory systems while preserving mathematical exactness in individual-tree reconstruction. In addition, the model supports flexible calibration schemes ranging from single-point (DBH-only) to multi-point configurations, depending on the availability of empirical diameter measurements.

A further distinguishing aspect of the proposed approach is the use of a general reference diameter rather than a fixed breast-height diameter. This formulation removes the dependence on DBH as a mandatory anchor point and provides a mathematically consistent basis across the full ontogenetic range of tree development, including juvenile trees below conventional breast-height thresholds.

Using a dataset comprising \num{15684} sampled stems from Switzerland, the model parameters are shown to vary systematically with standard inventory variables such as tree height and reference diameter. The resulting framework provides a computationally efficient, biologically interpretable, and analytically tractable basis for modern forest inventories, enabling consistent stem reconstruction from juvenile trees to mature timber.


\section{Data}

The analysis is based on the openly available dataset published by~\cite{Didion2024sectionWiseStemDiametersText,Didion2024sectionWiseStemDiametersData}, which provides detailed stem measurements for multiple tree species. From this dataset, Norway spruce (\textit{Picea abies}) was selected in order to ensure species-specific consistency in stem form modelling. The dataset comprises measurements from \num{15684} stems and was chosen not only due to its broad coverage of tree sizes and high-resolution sectional measurement design, but also because it is fully open and therefore enables reproducibility of the modelling framework and all derived results.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data Processing and Profile Completion}

\subsubsection{Terminal Diameter}

While mathematically it would be simpler to assume a terminal diameter of zero at the tree apex, this is biologically unrealistic: the apex corresponds to apical meristematic tissue with a finite diameter. To reflect this, a small, height-dependent terminal diameter $d_t$ is introduced. Empirically, the diameter of the terminal shoot is smaller in young trees and approaches an approximately constant value in larger individuals.  

The function is therefore defined as
\begin{equation}
d_t =
\begin{cases}
0.8 & \text{for } H_{\text{total}} \geq 1.3 \\
0.1 + 0.7 \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{1.3 - H_{\text{total}}}{1.3} \right)^2 \right] & \text{for } H_{\text{total}} < 1.3
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $d_t$ is the terminal diameter in \unit{cm} and $H_{\text{total}}$ is the total tree height in \unit{m}.

This choice preserves biological realism at the apex while having only a negligible effect on total stem volume or taper predictions.


\subsubsection{Reference Diameter}

The reference diameter ($d_r$) serves as a continuous replacement for the diameter at breast height (DBH), providing a consistent reference for stem profile reconstruction across the full range of tree sizes. For trees exceeding \qty{1.3}{m} in height, a constant offset of \qty{1.3}{cm} is added to the DBH, corresponding to a theoretical height-to-diameter ratio ($H/D$) of 100:

\begin{equation}
d_r =
\begin{cases}
\text{DBH} + 1.3 & \text{if } H_{\text{total}} \geq \qty{1.3}{m}, \\
d_t + H_{\text{total}} & \text{if } H_{\text{total}} < \qty{1.3}{m},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $d_r$ and DBH are expressed in \unit{cm} and $H_{\text{total}}$ in \unit{m}.

For trees shorter than \qty{1.3}{m}, the reference diameter is approximated directly from the total height. Although this height-dependent approximation does not explicitly account for diameter variation caused by stand density or competition in juvenile individuals, it provides a mathematically consistent size descriptor applicable across the entire dataset.


\subsubsection{Basal Diameter Reconstruction}

The reconstruction of the basal stem profile requires a consistent description of stem geometry below and near ground level, where measurements are sparse and strongly influenced by root flare. The proposed framework therefore combines local empirical fits with a global statistical model to obtain a continuous and biologically plausible representation of basal taper.

To stabilise the reconstruction at the stem base, a hypothetical diameter at \qty{1}{m} below ground level ($d_{-1m}$) is introduced as a boundary condition. This allows the basal stem curve to be defined not only within the range of observed measurements but also in the near-ground region, where direct observations are typically unavailable or affected by irregular root structures.

\paragraph{Local stem-specific reconstruction}

For a subset of \num{2828} trees with sufficient lower-stem measurements, basal taper was first described using tree-specific power functions:

\begin{equation}
d(h) = c_0 + c_1 \cdot (5 - h)^{c_2}
\end{equation}

where $d(h)$ denotes stem diameter at height $h$, and \qty{5}{m} defines the upper limit of the basal modelling domain. This domain was chosen to include both the lower stem and the transition region around the typical inflection point, while maintaining a sufficient density of observations for robust parameter estimation. The parameters $c_0$, $c_1$, and $c_2$ were fitted individually for each tree.

To ensure biological plausibility, individual fits were excluded if extrapolated diameters exceeded predefined thresholds relative to the reference diameter $d_r$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $d_0 > 2.0 \cdot d_r$ (40 cases rejected)
\item $d_{-1m} > 3.0 \cdot d_r$ (412 cases rejected)
\end{itemize}

These constraints prevent unrealistic basal swelling arising from unstable extrapolation in trees with limited near-ground measurements.

\paragraph{Generalised basal reconstruction model}

For trees for which no valid individual fit could be obtained, basal diameter reconstruction was performed using a Generalised Linear Model (GLM) with Gamma distribution and log-link function. This component captures population-level patterns in basal taper under heteroscedastic variability, which increases with tree size.

The basal taper intensity is expressed through the slope parameter $s_0$, defined as the diameter increment per unit height between the reference height $h_{\text{D}}$ and the ground:

\begin{equation}
h_{\text{D}} = \min(1.3, H_{\text{total}})
\end{equation}

with $d_{\text{D}}$ denoting the corresponding diameter at $h_{\text{D}}$ (either DBH or $d_t$).

To ensure a minimum biologically plausible taper, a baseline slope of \qty{0.015}{cm/cm} is assumed, and additional variation is modelled as:

\begin{equation}
\ln(s_0 - 0.015) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \frac{1}{H_{\text{total}}} + \beta_2 \cdot \frac{1}{d_{\text{D}}}
\end{equation}

The ground-level diameter is then reconstructed as:

\begin{equation}
d_0 = d_{\text{D}} + s_0 \cdot h_{\text{D}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Sub-ground extension}

To ensure continuity of the basal profile below ground level, an additional slope component $s_{-1m}$ is introduced. Rather than representing a separate biological process, this term extends the same basal taper behaviour into the sub-ground domain:

\begin{equation}
\ln(s_{-1m}) = \beta_3 + \beta_4 \cdot \frac{1}{H_{\text{total}}}
\end{equation}

The diameter at \qty{1}{m} below ground is obtained by consistent extension of the basal slope:

\begin{equation}
d_{-1m} = d_{\text{D}} + (s_0 + s_{-1m}) \cdot (h_{\text{D}} + 1)
\end{equation}


\subsection{Stem Profile}

Stem taper exhibits limited morphological variability across trees while remaining constrained within a characteristic range of shapes (Fig.~\ref{fig:relativeTaper}). To ensure biologically consistent reconstructions, monotonicity and taper constraints were imposed, requiring diameters to decrease monotonically with height and restricting local taper rates to physiologically plausible ranges. Deviations from these constraints may arise from measurement noise, branch whorls, or recording errors and can lead to distortions in volume estimation.

\subsubsection{Taper Rate and Curvature Analysis}

To characterise local stem geometry and identify potential measurement anomalies, a discrete taper rate $s_j$ and its rate of change $\Delta s_j$ were computed for each stem section. These quantities represent first- and second-order finite differences of the diameter profile.

Let $h_j$ and $d_j$ denote the ordered measurement heights and corresponding diameters, and let $\Delta h_j = h_{j+1} - h_j$ be the section length. The taper rate is defined at the section midpoint $\bar{h}_j$ as:

\begin{equation}
s_j = \frac{d_{j+1} - d_j}{\Delta h_j}, \quad \text{at relative height } z_j = \frac{\bar{h}_j}{H}
\end{equation}

where $H$ is total tree height. This formulation represents the local diameter gradient along the stem.

The change in taper rate is then approximated by a discrete second-order difference:

\begin{equation}
\Delta s_j = \frac{s_{j+1} - s_j}{\bar{h}_{j+1} - \bar{h}_j}, \quad \text{at relative height } \zeta_j = \frac{\bar{\bar{h}}_j}{H}
\end{equation}

The distribution of these quantities as a function of relative height is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:taper_corridor}. To characterise population-level behaviour across the dataset of \num{15684} stems, height-dependent quantile envelopes (2.5\% and 97.5\%) were computed using section lengths as weights. This ensures that vertically more extensive stem segments contribute proportionally to the statistical characterization.

The results indicate that extreme taper rates and curvature are primarily concentrated in the lower 15\% of tree height ($h/H < 0.15$), corresponding to the root flare region. Between 20\% and 80\% of tree height, both taper rate and curvature stabilise, reflecting a comparatively uniform mid-stem zone with near-parabolic geometry.

\subsubsection{Data Smoothing and Outlier Handling}

This step transforms raw stem measurements into biologically consistent profiles suitable for subsequent analytical reconstruction and volume integration. A constraint-based regularisation procedure was applied to enforce (i) monotonic decrease of diameter with height ($d_{j+1} \leq d_j$) and (ii) consistency with height-dependent plausibility bounds derived from the taper analysis.

When inconsistencies were detected, adjacent measurements were merged into a single representative point using a weighted centroid formulation:

\begin{equation}
h_{\text{new}} = \frac{h_i w_i + h_{i+1} w_{i+1}}{w_i + w_{i+1}}, \quad
d_{\text{new}} = \frac{d_i w_i + d_{i+1} w_{i+1}}{w_i + w_{i+1}}
\end{equation}

The weighting scheme prevents accumulation effects by avoiding increased influence of measurements that participate multiple times in the merging procedure.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:smoothing_example}, this procedure removes local oscillations and measurement noise while preserving the characteristic taper structure of the stem. Following regularisation, the reconstructed basal diameters ($d_0$ and $d_{-1\text{m}}$) were incorporated into the dataset to complete the stem profiles for subsequent reconstruction and volume estimation.

\subsubsection{Identification of the Inflection Point}

To characterise the transition from the basal root flare to the upper stem, the inflection point height ($H_{\text{ip}}$) was determined for each stem. This point corresponds to a morphologically defined transition between the basal flare region and the upper stem and is identified by a local extremum of the taper slope, where the rate of change of slope transitions from positive to negative values.

In the present study, the term inflection point denotes this taper-defined transition and does not necessarily coincide with the exact geometric inflection point of the diameter profile.

For each stem, the taper slope $s$ and its numerical derivative with respect to height $s' = \mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}H$ were approximated using central differences at the measured height intervals. The inflection point was initially located by identifying the section where the derivative changes sign ($s'_j > 0$ and $s'_{j+1} < 0$), under the condition that the curvature remains positive from the base to that point, ensuring a biologically plausible basal flare. The exact height was then obtained via linear interpolation between the bounding points:

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{ip}} = H_j - s'_j \cdot \frac{H_{j+1} - H_j}{s'_{j+1} - s'_j}.
\end{equation}

To describe the population-level relationship between the inflection point and total tree height ($H_{\text{total}}$), a non-linear model was fitted:

\begin{equation}
H_{\text{ip}} = a \cdot (1 + H_{\text{total}})^b - a,
\end{equation}

which ensures that the model passes through the origin ($H_{\text{ip}} = 0$ for $H_{\text{total}} = 0$) and captures the observed scaling of inflection height with tree size.


\subsection{Volume Calculation and Partitioning}

The total stem volume over bark ($V_{\text{total}}$) and its structural components were derived from the reconstructed reference stem profiles by treating the stem as a solid of revolution. To ensure a biologically realistic and strictly monotonic taper between measurement points, Steffen monotonic cubic spline interpolation was applied to the diameter--height pairs, incorporating the reconstructed basal values ($d_0, d_{-1\text{m}}$). This procedure was used solely for the construction and analysis of reference profiles; the final taper model itself retains fully analytical expressions for diameter, height, and volume.

The cross-sectional area over bark at height $h$ is defined as $A(h) = \frac{\pi}{4} d(h)^2$. The volume between two vertical positions is calculated via numerical integration:

\begin{equation}
  V = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \int_{h_{\text{start}}}^{h_{\text{end}}} d(h)^2 \, dh
\end{equation}

where the constant $40\,000$ accounts for the conversion of diameters (\unit{cm}) and heights (\unit{m}) into volume (\unit{m^3}). Numerical integration was performed using adaptive Gauss-Kronrod quadrature.

To further analyse the stem structure, the total volume was partitioned into two components based on the identified inflection point ($H_{\text{ip}}$). The \emph{basal volume} ($V_{basal}$) represents the portion of the stem between the ground ($h = 0$) and the inflection point ($h = H_{\text{ip}}$), capturing the root flare. The \emph{upper volume} ($V_{upper}$) comprises the remaining section from the inflection point to the tree tip ($h = H_{\text{total}}$).


\subsection{Analytical Derivation of the Stem Profile Function}

The stem profile is described by a composite model consisting of two power functions joined at the transition height, corresponding to the previously identified inflection point ($H_{\text{ip}}$). To ensure a biologically realistic and smooth connection, the model is constructed to be $C^1$-continuous (continuous in both diameter and its first derivative):

\begin{equation}
d(h) = 
\begin{cases} 
a(H_{\text{total}} - h)^b + d_t & \text{for } H_{\text{ip}} \le h \le H_{\text{total}}, \\
c_0 + c_1 h^{c_2} & \text{for } 0 \le h < H_{\text{ip}}, 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $h$ is the measurement height, $H_{\text{total}}$ is the total tree height, and $d_t$ is the terminal diameter.

In the first step, the upper segment ($h \ge H_{\text{ip}}$) is scaled to pass exactly through $d_{\text{ip}} = d(H_{\text{ip}})$ and $d_t$:

\begin{equation}
a = \frac{d_{\text{ip}} - d_t}{(H_{\text{total}} - H_{\text{ip}})^b}, \quad
s_{\text{ip}} = -a \cdot b \cdot (H_{\text{total}} - H_{\text{ip}})^{b-1}
\end{equation}

where $s_{\text{ip}}$ is the slope at the transition height.

In the second step, the basal segment ($h < H_{\text{ip}}$) is defined to satisfy $C^1$-continuity. With $c_0 = d_0$ (ground-level diameter), the exponent and scaling parameter are determined as:

\begin{equation}
c_2 = \frac{s_{\text{ip}} \cdot H_{\text{ip}}}{d_{\text{ip}} - d_0}, \quad
c_1 = \frac{d_{\text{ip}} - d_0}{H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Modeling Shape Exponents using Generalized Linear Models}

To estimate the stem profile for trees where only height ($H$) and reference diameter ($d_r$) are available, the shape exponents $b$ (upper shaft) and $c_2$ (basal swell) were modeled using Generalized Linear Models (GLMs). Since both parameters are theoretically and empirically bounded between 0 and 1, a logit link function was employed. To ensure that all predicted values $\theta \in \{b, c_2\}$ remain within this biologically plausible range, the model is expressed via the logistic function:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:glm_general}
\theta = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot f(H) + \beta_2 \cdot g(d_r)\right)\right)}
\end{equation}

The optimal functional forms for the predictors were determined by analyzing residual distributions and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). For the upper shaft exponent $b$, an inverse transformation of height and a logarithmic transformation of the reference diameter provided the best fit:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:glm_exponent_b}
b = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left(\beta_{b,0} + \beta_{b,1} \frac{1}{H} + \beta_{b,2} \ln(1 + d_r)\right)\right)}
\end{equation}

For the basal exponent $c_2$, which describes the intensity of the root flare, a logarithmic transformation for both height and diameter was selected:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:glm_exponent_c2}
c_2 = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left(\beta_{c_2,0} + \beta_{c_2,1} \ln(1 + H) + \beta_{c_2,2} \ln(1 + d_r)\right)\right)}
\end{equation}

In standard field inventories, the reference diameter is directly derived from the measured breast-height diameter ($\text{DBH}$), setting $d_r = \text{DBH} + 1.3$ for trees where $H \geq \qty{1.3}{m}$. However, under advanced multi-diameter sampling frameworks or remote sensing applications where a direct physical measurement at $h = \qty{1.3}{m}$ is missing, the system of equations forms an implicit problem. 

Because \cref{eq:glm_exponent_b,eq:glm_exponent_c2} establish a non-linear co-dependency between the shape exponents, the basal taper geometry, and the reference diameter itself, $d_r$ cannot be solved analytically in the absence of $\text{DBH}$. In such scenarios, the model architecture treats $d_r$ as an unobserved structural state variable. The missing metric is reconstructed by embedding the GLM formulations within an outer numerical root-finding loop. This routine iteratively converges on an implicit reference diameter ($d_{r,\text{implicit}}$) that guarantees mathematical consistency between the predicted taper trajectory at breast height and the underlying regional biometric priors.



\subsection{Model Anchoring and Parameterisation}

To ensure high operational flexibility across varying data, the composite taper model can adaptively adjusts its parameterisation routine based on the number of measured diameters along the stem, $n_{\text{pts}}$, available for a given tree. The model architecture seamlessly scales from a fully deterministic, constraint-bound geometry in the complete absence of empirical diameter data ($n_{\text{pts}} = 0$) to a strictly anchored profile when a single reference point is provided ($n_{\text{pts}} = 1$). For higher data densities ($n_{\text{pts}} > 1$), the framework transitions into a calibrated optimisation scheme. 

In each configuration, the system balances regional shape characteristics derived from generalised linear models (GLMs) with local geometric constraints. This variable-density anchoring approach ensures that the resulting taper curves remain mathematically continuous ($C^1$-smooth), biologically realistic, and rigorously conditioned against structural anomalies such as extreme basal swelling or profile inversion.

\subsubsection{Case 1: $n_{\text{pts}} = 0$ (Height-Only Estimation)}

For assets where no empirical diameter measurements are available ($n_{\text{pts}} = 0$, H < \qty{1.3}{m}), a standard geometric anchor point is missing. To resolve the system, a theoretical baseline diameter $c_{0,\text{local}}$ is defined at the stem base, based on the terminal tip diameter $d_t$ and total height $H$, but strictly capped by an upper biological threshold $c_{0,\text{limit}}$:
\begin{equation}
    c_{0,\text{local}} = \min(d_t + H, c_{0,\text{limit}})
\end{equation}

With $c_{0,\text{local}}$ fixed, the remaining profile parameters must be resolved to satisfy both the regional shape exponents ($b, c_2$) and the condition of $C^1$-continuity at the inflection height $H_{ip}$. Since the transition diameter $d_{ip}$ at $H_{ip}$ cannot be derived analytically, a numerical root-finding procedure via the bisection method is employed to isolate the upper scaling parameter $a$. 

The search space for $a$ is bounded by $a_{\text{low}}$ and $a_{\text{high}}$. For each candidate value of $a$, the interface parameters at $H_{ip}$ are sequentially evaluated:
\begin{equation}
    d_{ip}(a) = a \cdot (H - H_{ip})^b + d_t
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_{ip}(a) = -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{ip})^{b-1}
\end{equation}
Enforcing a smooth transition at the inflection point yields the implied parameters for the basal segment:
\begin{equation}
    c_1(a) = \frac{s_{ip}(a)}{c_2 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2-1}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    c_{0,\text{theoretical}}(a) = d_{ip}(a) - c_1(a) \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2}
\end{equation}

The algorithm iteratively updates the boundaries of $a$ until the residual error $\epsilon = c_{0,\text{theoretical}} - c_{0,\text{local}}$ converges. Once $a$ is established, the profile is uniquely defined, ensuring a smooth and monotonically decreasing taper curve.


\subsubsection{Case 2: $n_{\text{pts}} = 1$ (Single Reference Point Anchoring)}

To ensure practical applicability, the composite taper model must be anchored to a single empirical diameter measurement ($d_1$) taken at an arbitrary reference height ($h_1$), forcing the profile curve strictly through this fixed point $(h_1, d_1)$. This formulation generalises the anchoring routine, accommodating traditional diameter at breast height (DBH, where $h_1 = \qty{1.3}{m}$) as well as any other user-defined sample point. Depending on the tree dimensions and the relative positioning of the measurement height $h_1$ to the transition boundary $H_{ip}$, two distinct scenarios are resolved.

\paragraph{Case 2a: $h_1 \le H_{ip}$ (Anchoring within the Basal Segment)}

When the empirical reference height lies within the lower segment below or at the inflection point, the parameters $c_0$, $c_1$, and the transition diameter $d_{ip} = d(H_{ip})$ must be solved simultaneously. To preserve full $C^1$-continuity, the basal function must intersect the coordinates $(h_1, d_1)$ while smoothly matching both the diameter and the slope of the upper shaft model at $H_{ip}$. 

The continuous interface slope is analytically defined as the first derivative of the upper segment evaluated at $H_{ip}$:
\begin{equation}
    s_{ip} = -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{ip})^{b-1}
\end{equation}
Substituting the upper scaling parameter $a = (d_{ip} - d_t) / (H - H_{ip})^b$ yields $s_{ip}$ as a linear function of the unknown transition diameter $d_{ip}$:
\begin{equation}
    s_{ip} = -(d_{ip} - d_t) \cdot \frac{b}{H - H_{ip}}
\end{equation}
Enforcing the $C^1$-continuity constraints into the lower power function requires that the anchor point satisfies:
\begin{equation}
    d_1 = d_{ip} - \frac{s_{ip} \cdot H_{ip}}{c_2} \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{h_1}{H_{ip}} \right)^{c_2} \right]
\end{equation}
By substituting the linear expression for $s_{ip}$ into this boundary condition, the system reduces to a single implicit equation in $d_{ip}$ that can be evaluated without iterative root-finding. $d_{ip}$ is isolated by introducing the structural scaling factor $\Omega$:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega = \frac{b \cdot H_{ip}}{c_2 \cdot (H - H_{ip})} \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{h_1}{H_{ip}} \right)^{c_2} \right]
\end{equation}
The unique trial transition diameter is then deterministically computed as:
\begin{equation}
    d_{ip,\text{trial}} = \frac{d_1 + d_t \cdot \Omega}{1 + \Omega}
\end{equation}
from which the remaining profile parameters ($a, s_{ip}, c_1, c_0$) are resolved sequentially via back-substitution. If this initial configuration violates the geometric constraints detailed below, the analytical assumption of fixed regional exponents is relaxed, and $c_2$ is optimised numerically via a bisection method until the system converges to the exact baseline boundary.

\paragraph{Case 2b: $h_1 > H_{ip}$ (Anchoring within the Upper Segment)}

If the reference measurement height occurs strictly above the inflection point, the anchor point resides entirely within the upper shaft segment. The scaling parameter $a$ can therefore be directly isolated from the upper power function using the measured coordinates $(h_1, d_1)$:
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{d_1 - d_t}{(H - h_1)^b}
\end{equation}
To ensure smooth $C^1$-continuity across the profile, the downstream basal parameters are subsequently determined in a strict sequential order:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The transition diameter: $d_{ip} = a(H - H_{ip})^b + d_t$
    \item The transition slope: $s_{ip} = -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{ip})^{b-1}$
    \item The basal scaling parameter: $c_1 = \frac{s_{ip}}{c_2 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2-1}}$
    \item The basal intercept: $c_0 = d_{ip} - c_1 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2}$
\end{enumerate}
In the event that the unconstrained intercept exceeds the biological upper bound, $c_0$ is truncated to the maximum allowed limit, and $c_2$ is updated analytically via an algebraic clamping function to restore continuity.

\subsubsection{Case 3: $n_{\text{pts}} = 2$ (Two-Point Calibration)}

When two empirical diameter measurements over bark, $(h_1, d_1)$ and $(h_2, d_2)$, are available, the profile can be calibrated to capture tree-specific taper variations, reducing reliance on regional $\text{GLM}$ predictions. The calibration routine depends entirely on the positioning of these points relative to the inflection height $H_{\text{ip}}$:

\paragraph{Case 3a: Both measurements within the upper segment ($h_1, h_2 \ge H_{\text{ip}}$)}
When both observations lie on the upper shaft, the two parameters of the upper segment are uniquely determined. The local shape exponent $b$ and the scaling parameter $a$ can be analytically isolated via log-linear extraction:
\begin{equation}
    b = \text{clamp}\left( \frac{\ln\left( \frac{d_1 - d_t}{d_2 - d_t} \right)}{\ln\left( \frac{H - h_1}{H - h_2} \right)}, \, 0.1, \, 0.95 \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{d_1 - d_t}{(H - h_1)^b},
\end{equation}
where the heights and diameters are automatically sorted such that $h_1 > h_2$. The tree-specific exponent $b$ replaces the regional $\text{GLM}$ prediction. The transition diameter $d_{\text{ip}}$ and local slope $s_{\text{ip}}$ are subsequently calculated by evaluating this upper power function at $H_{\text{ip}}$. To enforce $C^1$-continuity down to the lower stem base, the basal coefficients resolve sequentially to:
\begin{equation}
    c_1 = \frac{s_{\text{ip}}}{c_2 \cdot H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2-1}}, 
    \quad \text{and} \quad c_0 = d_{\text{ip}} - c_1 H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2},
\end{equation}
where $c_2$ is initially set to the regional expectation $c_{2,\text{GLM}}$. If the resulting basal intercept exceeds the biological threshold ($c_0 > c_{0,\text{limit}}$), the exponent is adjusted to $c_2 = 2.0$ and the coefficients $c_1$ and $c_0$ are re-calculated to prevent a base-diameter explosion while strictly preserving $C^1$-continuity.

\paragraph{Case 3b: Measurements split across segments ($h_1 < H_{\text{ip}} \le h_2$)}
This configuration typically occurs when $h_1$ represents the standard $\text{DBH}$ field measurement and $h_2$ is an upper-stem diameter. Here, the system acts as a coupled bridge. Because the upper scaling parameter $a$ is structurally bound to the upper measurement $(h_2, d_2)$, the shape exponent $b$ becomes the primary degree of freedom to link both segments smoothly.

A numerical root-finding procedure via the bisection method is employed to optimise $b$ within the stable domain $[0.05, 1.95]$ by minimising the trajectory mismatch at the lower anchor point:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(b) = \left[ c_{0,\text{trial}}(b) + c_{1,\text{trial}}(b) \cdot h_1^{c_{2,\text{GLM}}} \right] - d_1,
\end{equation}
where each candidate value of $b$ sequentially induces a trial path for $a$, $d_{\text{ip}}$, and $s_{\text{ip}}$, which in turn dictate the dependent lower parameters $c_{1,\text{trial}}$ and $c_{0,\text{trial}}$ via $C^1$-continuity links. Upon convergence, the continuous profile is locked, satisfying both empirical data points while preserving $C^1$-continuity.

\paragraph{Case 3c: Both measurements within the basal segment ($h_1, h_2 < H_{\text{ip}}$)}
When both observations are located near the stem base, they reflect the geometry of the root flare rather than the upper canopy. In this configuration, the main-shaft geometry cannot be derived directly from the measurements. Therefore, the upper shape exponent is anchored to its regional biometric expectation ($b = b_{\text{GLM}}$). To resolve the remaining profile geometry, a one-dimensional numerical optimisation is performed on the basal shape parameter $c_2$.

For any candidate value of $c_2$, the basal coefficients $c_0$ and $c_1$ are analytically determined by solving the linear system formed by the two lower measurements:
\begin{equation}
    c_1(c_2) = \frac{d_2 - d_1}{h_2^{c_2} - h_1^{c_2}},
    \quad \text{and} \quad c_0(c_2) = d_1 - c_1(c_2) \cdot h_1^{c_2}.
\end{equation}
These coefficients uniquely define the implied transition diameter $d_{\text{ip}}$ and the actual slope $s_{\text{ip}}$ at the inflection height:
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ip}}(c_2) = c_0(c_2) + c_1(c_2) \cdot H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{ip}}(c_2) = c_1(c_2) \cdot c_2 \cdot H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2-1}.
\end{equation}
The optimisation routine searches for the optimal exponent $c_2$ within the stable domain $[0.1, 1.5]$ using Brent's method to minimise the slope mismatch (kink) at the transition interface against the expected upper canopy slope:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{c_2} \left[ s_{\text{ip}}(c_2) - s_{\text{expected}}(c_2) \right]^2,
\end{equation}
where the expected upper derivative is conditionally bound via the continuity-driven scale parameter $a(c_2) = (d_{\text{ip}}(c_2) - d_t) / (H - H_{\text{ip}})^{b_{\text{GLM}}}$, yielding:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{expected}}(c_2) = -a(c_2) \cdot b_{\text{GLM}} \cdot (H - H_{\text{ip}})^{b_{\text{GLM}}-1}.
\end{equation}
Once $c_2$ is optimised, the remaining upper parameter $a$ is locked to match the transition point smoothly.


\subsubsection{Case 4: $n_{\text{pts}} = 3$ (Three-Point Full Calibration)}
When exactly three empirical diameter measurements are available, the profile parameters can be completely calibrated without forcing a rigid anchor point. Instead, a simultaneous three-dimensional numerical optimisation is employed to fit the entire stem curve globally. To prevent geometric anomalies, the system estimates the shape exponents within strict biological boundaries using a bounded box algorithm (\textit{Fminbox} with Nelder-Mead), ensuring the profile remains structurally sound.

Prior to optimisation, the three empirical pairs are sorted strictly by ascending height to guarantee consistent geometric mapping. The routine then branches based on the spatial distribution of the measurements relative to the inflection height $H_{\text{ip}}$:

\paragraph{Case 4a: Normal Configuration (Distributed Measurements)}
When the measurements are distributed both below and above $H_{\text{ip}}$, a free three-dimensional search is executed over the parameter vector $\mathbf{x} = [b, a, c_2]^T$. For any candidate vector, $C^1$-continuity at the transition interface is strictly maintained. The transition diameter $d_{\text{ip}}$ and the local slope $s_{\text{ip}}$ are determined directly by the upper canopy parameters:
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ip}}(b, a) = a \cdot (H - H_{\text{ip}})^b + d_t,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_{\text{ip}}(b, a) = -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{\text{ip}})^{b-1}.
\end{equation}
These state variables subsequently isolate the dependent lower basal coefficients analytically:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    c_1(b, a, c_2) &= \frac{s_{\text{ip}}(b, a)}{c_2 \cdot H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2-1}}, \\
    \intertext{and}
    c_0(b, a, c_2) &= d_{\text{ip}}(b, a) - c_1(b, a, c_2) \cdot H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
To prevent unphysical, hourglass-shaped stem bases in irregular data configurations, a biological boundary condition is enforced on the basal intercept: if $c_0 > c_{0,\text{limit}}$, it is capped at $c_{0,\text{limit}}$ and $c_1$ is updated via $c_1 = (d_{\text{ip}} - c_{0,\text{limit}}) / H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2}$. Conversely, if $c_0 < 0$, it is restricted to zero and $c_1 = d_{\text{ip}} / H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2}$. 

The optimal vector is found by minimising the sum of squared absolute residuals of the cross-sectional areas (proportional to $d^2$), which directly mirrors the target volume minimisation within the stable domain $\mathbf{x} \in [0.05, 1.5] \times [10^{-4}, 2000.0] \times [0.01, 15.0]$:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{b, a, c_2} \sum_{i=1}^{3} \left( d_{\text{model}}^2(h_i; b, a, c_2) - d_i^2 \right)^2,
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{model}}(h)$ evaluates the respective segment function depending on whether $h \le H_{\text{ip}}$.

\paragraph{Case 4b: Boundary Configurations (All Points on One Side)}
If the structural data configuration lacks spatial distribution across the inflection point, the system automatically adapts to maintain mathematical determinacy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{All measurements below $H_{\text{ip}}$:} The routine organises a 3D search over $\mathbf{x} = [b, c_2, c_0]^T$. The unmeasured upper segment scale $a$ and coefficient $c_1$ are analytically derived from the continuity interface constraints toward the asymptotic tip diameter $d_t$.
    \item \textbf{All measurements above $H_{\text{ip}}$:} The routine executes a 3D search over $\mathbf{x} = [b, a, c_2]^T$. The unmeasured basal parameters $c_0$ and $c_1$ are projected downward from the inflection point interface. An initial dynamic guess for $a$ is derived from the first upper observation to guarantee fast convergence.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Geometric Constraints and Biological Bounds}
To prevent unrealistic basal expansion and ensure structural stability across all anchoring configurations, an upper bound on the theoretical ground-level diameter is established:
\begin{equation}
    c_{0,\text{limit}} = 1.8 \cdot d_{\text{ref}}
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference diameter (derived from the field $\text{DBH}$ or tree height $H$). This constraint strictly limits excessive root flare development in underdetermined scenarios, while preserving sufficient mathematical flexibility to accommodate size-dependent taper variations throughout the model pipeline.


\subsection{Analytical Applications: Diameters, Heights, and Volumes}

The mathematical structure of the composite power function allows for direct analytical solutions for both the height of a specific diameter and the integrated volume.

\subsubsection{Height of a Given Diameter}

To determine the height $h(d)$ for a target diameter $d$, the profile function is inverted. Depending on whether the target diameter lies within the basal or the upper segment, the height is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:height_at_diameter}
h(d) = 
\begin{cases} 
H_{\text{total}} - \left( \frac{d - d_t}{a} \right)^{1/b} & \text{for } d < d_{ip} \text{ (upper segment)} \\
\left( \frac{d - c_0}{c_1} \right)^{1/c_2} & \text{for } d \ge d_{ip} \text{ (basal segment)} 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

This inverse relationship is particularly useful for assessing commercial assortments, such as determining the height at a specific merchantable top diameter.

\subsubsection{Analytical Volume Integration}

The volume $V$ of a stem segment between two heights $h_1$ and $h_2$ is calculated by integrating the cross-sectional area: $V = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \int_{h_1}^{h_2} d(h)^2 \, dh$, where the constant $40\,000$ accounts for the standard conversion of units from cm$^2\cdot$m to m$^3$. Since the profile consists of two segments, the integral is solved piecewise.

For the \textbf{upper segment} ($h \ge H_{\text{ip}}$), substituting $z = (H_{\text{total}}-h)$ simplifies the integration. Due to the change of variables ($dh = -dz$), the limits of integration invert. Substituting $z$ back after integration yields the expression:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{up}}(h_1, h_2) = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Bigg[ & d_t^2 h + \frac{2 a d_t (H_{\text{total}}-h)^{b+1}}{b+1} \\
& - \frac{a^2 (H_{\text{total}}-h)^{2b+1}}{2b+1} \Bigg]_{h_1}^{h_2}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For the special case of calculating the volume from the transition height $H_{\text{ip}}$ to the total height $H_{\text{total}}$, the expression simplifies significantly as the terms containing $(H_{\text{total}}-H_{\text{total}})$ vanish at the upper limit:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  V_{\text{up}}&(H_{\text{ip}}, H_{\text{total}}) =  \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Big[ d_t^2 (H_{\text{total}} - H_{\text{ip}}) \\
 &  +  \frac{2 a d_t (H_{\text{total}}-H_{\text{ip}})^{b+1}}{b+1} 
   + \frac{a^2 (H_{\text{total}}-H_{\text{ip}})^{2b+1}}{2b+1} \Big]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the practical implementation, the volume of any segment $[h_1, h_2]$ is computed by evaluating the antiderivative $F(h)$ at both boundaries: $V = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} (F(h_2) - F(h_1))$. This analytical approach is computationally efficient and avoids numerical approximation errors.


For the \textbf{basal segment} ($h < H_{\text{ip}}$), squaring and integrating the power function term by term yields:

\begin{equation}
V_{\text{low}}(h_1, h_2) = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \left[ c_0^2 h + \frac{2c_0 c_1 h^{c_2+1}}{c_2+1} + \frac{c_1^2 h^{2c_2+1}}{2c_2+1} \right]_{h_1}^{h_2}
\end{equation}

For the specific case of the lower integration limit starting at the ground ($h_1 = 0$), the terms vanish, and the volume depends solely on the evaluation at the upper boundary.

The total volume over bark $V_{\text{total}}$ is obtained by summing the integrals over their respective domains: $V_{\text{low}}(0, H_{\text{ip}}) + V_{\text{up}}(H_{\text{ip}}, H_{\text{total}})$. For any specific assortment, the boundaries $h_1$ and $h_2$ are derived using the inverse diameter function and a known stump height.

\subsection{Bark Thickness and Volume under Bark}

To isolate the commercial wood volume ($V_{\text{ub}}$, volume under bark), the double bark thickness ($\text{DBT}$) must be subtracted from the predicted diameter over bark ($d_{\text{ob}}$). The double bark thickness (\unit{cm}) along the stem is modelled as a function of the local diameter over bark ($d_{\text{ob}}$ in \unit{cm}) following \citet{roessler2008}:

\begin{equation}
    \text{DBT}(d_{\text{ob}}) = \frac{0.85149 + 0.60934 \cdot d_{\text{ob}} - 0.00228 \cdot d_{\text{ob}}^2}{10}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the divisor $10$ converts the original model output from millimetres to centimetres. Consequently, the local continuous diameter under bark ($d_{\text{ub}}$ in \unit{cm}) is calculated at any given height $h$ as:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ub}}(h) = d_{\text{ob}}(h) - \text{DBT}(d_{\text{ob}}(h))
\end{equation}

The actual wood volume without bark ($V_{\text{wood}}$) between two vertical positions $h_1$ and $h_2$ is determined by integrating the under-bark cross-sectional area:

\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{wood}} = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \int_{h_1}^{h_2} d_{\text{ub}}(h)^2 \, dh
\end{equation}

The total bark volume ($V_{\text{bark}}$) contained within the specified section is subsequently quantified as the difference between the total volume over bark ($V_{\text{ob}}$) and the volume under bark:

\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{bark}} = V_{\text{ob}} - V_{\text{wood}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Analytical Volume Integration Under Bark}

An analytical solution for the volume under bark ($V_{\text{wood}}$) is derived by expanding the underlying functions. Let the double bark thickness function be expressed generally as $\text{DBT}(d_{\text{ob}}) = \alpha + \beta d_{\text{ob}} + \gamma d_{\text{ob}}^2$, where $\alpha = 0.085149$, $\beta = 0.060934$, and $\gamma = -0.000228$. The diameter under bark can then be formulated as a polynomial function of the local diameter over bark:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ub}}(h) = (1-\beta)d_{\text{ob}}(h) - \gamma d_{\text{ob}}(h)^2 - \alpha
\end{equation}

Squaring this expression yields a fourth-order polynomial in terms of $d_{\text{ob}}(h)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{\text{ub}}(h)^2 = \,& A_0 + A_1 d_{\text{ob}}(h) + A_2 d_{\text{ob}}(h)^2 \\
& + A_3 d_{\text{ob}}(h)^3 + A_4 d_{\text{ob}}(h)^4
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where the constant coefficients $A_i$ are defined by expanding the trinomial expression:

\begin{align}
    A_0 &= \alpha^2 \\
    A_1 &= -2\alpha(1-\beta) \\
    A_2 &= (1-\beta)^2 + 2\alpha\gamma \\
    A_3 &= -2\gamma(1-\beta) \\
    A_4 &= \gamma^2
\end{align}

The volume for any stem segment is obtained by substituting the respective segment function for $d_{\text{ob}}(h)$ into the polynomial expansion and integrating term by term.

For the \textbf{upper segment} ($h \ge H_{\text{ip}}$), the substitution $z = H_{\text{total}} - h$ is applied to the upper profile function $d_{\text{ob}}(z) = a z^b + d_t$. Using a binomial expansion for $d_{\text{ob}}(z)^n$ yields:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ob}}(z)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} d_t^{n-k} a^k z^{k \cdot b}
\end{equation}

Accounting for the change of variables ($dh = -dz$), the exact antiderivative $F_{\text{up}}(z)$ is formulated such that the transformation maintains consistency with the directional integration boundaries:

\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{up}}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{4} A_n \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} d_t^{n-k} a^k \frac{z^{k \cdot b + 1}}{k \cdot b + 1} \right]
\end{equation}

The upper wood volume between heights $h_1$ and $h_2$ ($h_1 \ge H_{\text{ip}}$) is subsequently evaluated using the transformed boundaries $z_1 = H_{\text{total}} - h_1$ and $z_2 = H_{\text{total}} - h_2$:

\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{wood, up}}(h_1, h_2) = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \left[ F_{\text{up}}(z_2) - F_{\text{up}}(z_1) \right]
\end{equation}

For the \textbf{basal segment} ($h < H_{\text{ip}}$), the diameter is defined by $d_{\text{ob}}(h) = c_0 + c_1 h^{c_2}$. The powers of $d_{\text{ob}}(h)$ are expanded binomially:

\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{ob}}(h)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} c_0^{n-k} c_1^k h^{k \cdot c_2}
\end{equation}

Substituting these expansions into the volume integral yields the exact antiderivative $F_{\text{low}}(h)$ for the basal section:

\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{low}}(h) = \sum_{n=0}^{4} A_n \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} c_0^{n-k} c_1^k \frac{h^{k \cdot c_2 + 1}}{k \cdot c_2 + 1} \right]
\end{equation}

The basal wood volume between heights $h_1$ and $h_2$ ($h_2 \le H_{\text{ip}}$) is then calculated directly as:

\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{wood, low}}(h_1, h_2) = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \left[ F_{\text{low}}(h_2) - F_{\text{low}}(h_1) \right]
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stem Profile and Inflection Point}

The numerical identification of the inflection point $H_{\text{ip}}$ revealed a consistent morphological transition across the dataset. The fitted model, with its significant parameter estimates summarized in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients_shape_models}, is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\text{ip}} = 1.8104 \cdot (1 + H_{\text{total}})^{0.3590} - 1.8104
\end{equation}
This functional form demonstrates two critical biomathematical properties that ensure structural realism across all growth stages. First, the predicted inflection height is strictly monotonically increasing with total tree height ($\partial H_{\text{ip}} / \partial H_{\text{total}} > 0$). Second, the value of $H_{ip}$ is strictly bounded below the total tree height ($H_{\text{ip}} < H_{\text{total}}$) for all $H_{\text{total}} > 0$, preventing any structural collapse near the tip. 

The resulting parameters indicate that the inflection point height increases with total tree height at a decreasing rate, reflecting the relative stabilization of the root flare as trees mature.

Consequently, while the absolute height of the basal flare increases with tree size, its relative position on the stem shifts downwards as trees mature. The residuals exhibit no systematic trend across the predicted values, total heights, or reference diameters (Figure~\ref{fig:residualsHInfl}), confirming the robust generalization of the non-linear origin-enforced framework.

\subsection{Shape Exponents}

The partitioning of the stem volume into basal and upper components allowed for a estimation of the shape exponents $b$ and $c_2$. The GLM analysis confirmed that both parameters are significantly influenced by tree dimensions, with all calculated parameters summarized in Table~\ref{tab:coefficients_shape_models} ($p < 0.001$ for all coefficients).

The estimated coefficients indicate that the root flare becomes more pronounced as trees grow taller (indicated by a decreasing $c_2$), while an increase in reference diameter tends to moderate this effect. Residual analysis confirmed that the models are free of systematic bias across the entire range of sampled tree dimensions.

Crucially, because both parameters are statistically and biometrically restricted to the interval $(0, 1]$, the model guarantees a biologically realistic taper curvature. The upper segment is mathematically bound to a \emph{convex, parabolic profile}, while the basal segment exhibits a \emph{concave, neiloidic expansion} near the ground level.

\subsubsection{Upper Shaft Exponent $b$}
The exponent $b$, governing the upper stem profile, was primarily driven by the inverse of total height ($1/H$). The negative intercept combined with a strong positive coefficient for $1/H$ indicates that smaller trees exhibit a more conical shape, which transitions towards a more full-bodied, convex parabolic form in larger individuals. The reference diameter $d_{r}$ showed a moderate positive effect, suggesting a slight increase in $b$ for thicker stems. \subsection{Diameter Estimation Performance}

Figure~\ref{fig:d03Est} illustrates the residual analysis for the diameter estimation at $30\%$ of the tree height ($d_{0.3h}$). To account for heteroscedasticity---as absolute errors naturally increase with tree size---the ratio of the observed diameter to the predicted diameter ($d_{0.3h} / \hat{d}_{0.3h}$) is analyzed instead of absolute residuals. This approach scales the errors and provides a robust, size-independent standard deviation of $\sigma \approx 0.045$.

The model demonstrates high overall precision, with the vast majority of predictions deviating by less than $\pm 5\%$ from the actual values. However, the red trend line reveals a slight systematic bias across the diameter spectrum:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-to-medium diameters ($\hat{d}_{0.3h} < 25$\,cm):} The ratio lies slightly above $1.0$, indicating a minor underestimation of the actual diameter.
    \item \textbf{Large diameters ($\hat{d}_{0.3h} > 30$\,cm):} The trend line drops below $1.0$, signaling a progressive overestimation for thicker stems.
\end{itemize}
This behavior points toward a geometric constraint or a minor structural limitation in the taper equation. Despite this slight trend, the model remains robust and tightly clustered around the ideal baseline of $1.0$ across the bulk of the data.

\section{Numerical Illustration of Model Application}

To facilitate implementation, this section provides a step-by-step calculation for a Norway spruce with $\text{DBH} = \qty{30}{cm}$ and $H_{\text{total}} = \qty{20}{m}$.

\subsection{Reference Parameters}

\paragraph{Terminal Diameter ($d_t$):}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  d_t &= \begin{cases} 
    0.8 & H_{\text{total}} \geq 1.3 \\ 
    f(H_{\text{total}}) & H_{\text{total}} < 1.3 
  \end{cases} \\
  &\implies d_t = \qty{0.8}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\begin{footnotesize}
where $f(H_{\text{total}}) = 0.1 + 0.7 [ 1 - ( \frac{1.3 - H}{1.3} )^2 ]$.
\end{footnotesize}

\paragraph{Reference Diameter ($d_r$):}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  d_r &= \begin{cases} 
    \text{DBH} + 1.3 & H_{\text{total}} \geq 1.3 \\ 
    d_t + H_{\text{total}} & H_{\text{total}} < 1.3 
  \end{cases} \\
  &= 30 + 1.3 = \qty{31.3}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Shape Exponents}

\paragraph{Inflection Point Height ($H_{ip}$):}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  H_{ip} &= 1.8104 \cdot (1 + 20)^{0.3590} - 1.8104 \\
  &\approx 1.8 \cdot 3 - 1.8 = \qty{3.6}{m}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Exponents $b$ and $c_2$:}
First, calculate the logit values, then transform back to the unit interval:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{logit}(b) &= -4.3162 + 21.7497 \cdot \frac{1}{20} + 1.0813 \cdot \ln(1 + 31.3) \\
  &= -4.3162 + 1.0875 + 3.7576 = 0.5289 \\
  &\implies b = \frac{e^{0.5289}}{1 + e^{0.5289}} \approx 0.6292
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{logit}(c_2) &= 7.0974 - 3.1716 \cdot \ln(1 + 20) + 0.6313 \cdot \ln(1 + 31.3) \\
  &= 7.0974 - 9.6560 + 2.1938 = -0.3648 \\
  &\implies c_2 = \frac{e^{--0.3648}}{1 + e^{-0.3648}} \approx 0.4098
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Solving for the Stem Profile Parameters}

Since the DBH measurement (\(h_1=\qty{1.3}{m}\)) lies below the inflection point (\(H_{ip}=\qty{3.6}{m}\)), the example corresponds to Case 2a (\(h_1 \le H_{ip}\)). The transition diameter \(d_{ip}\) can therefore be obtained analytically.

\paragraph{Structural Scaling Factor}

\begin{equation}
\Omega
=
\frac{bH_{ip}}
     {c_2(H-H_{ip})}
\left[
1-\left(\frac{h_1}{H_{ip}}\right)^{c_2}
\right]
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Omega
&=
\frac{0.6292 \cdot 3.6}
     {0.4098 \cdot (20-3.6)}
\left[
1-\left(\frac{1.3}{3.6}\right)^{0.4098}
\right] \\
&= 0.1150
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Transition Diameter}

\begin{equation}
d_{ip}
=
\frac{d_1+d_t\Omega}
     {1+\Omega}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{ip}
&=
\frac{30 + 0.8 \cdot 0.1150}
     {1 + 0.1150} \\
&=
\qty{26.99}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Upper Segment Parameters}

\begin{equation}
a
=
\frac{d_{ip}-d_t}
     {(H-H_{ip})^b}
=
\frac{26.99-0.8}
     {(20-3.6)^{0.6292}}
=
4.5053
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{ip}
&=
-a\,b\,(H-H_{ip})^{b-1} \\
&=
-4.5053 \cdot 0.6292 \cdot (20 - 3.6)^{0.6292 - 1} \\
&=
-1.0047
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Basal Segment Parameters}

\begin{equation}
c_1
=
\frac{s_{ip}}
     {c_2 H_{ip}^{c_2-1}}
=
\frac{-1.0047}{0.4098 \cdot 3.6^{0.4098-1}}
=
\frac{-1.0047}{0.4098 \cdot 3.6^{-0.5902}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
3.6^{-0.5902} \approx 0.4877
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
c_1 = \frac{-1.0047}{0.4098 \cdot 0.4877} 
      = \frac{-1.0047}{0.1998} 
      \approx -5.2216
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
c_0
=
d_{ip}
-
c_1 H_{ip}^{c_2}
=
26.9880
-
(-5.2216 \cdot 3.6^{0.4098})
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
3.6^{0.4098} \approx 1.701
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
c_0 = 26.9880 - (-5.2216 \cdot 1.701)
      = 26.9880 + 8.826
      \approx 35.814
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Verification of Continuity}

\paragraph{Diameter continuity at $H_{ip}$:}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{\text{basal}}(H_{ip})
&= d_0 + c_1 H_{ip}^{c_2} \\
&= 35.81 + (-5.2216 \cdot 3.6^{0.4098}) 
\approx 26.99\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
d_{\text{upper}}(H_{ip})
&= a (H - H_{ip})^b + d_t \\
&= 4.5053 \cdot 16.4^{0.6292} + 0.8 
\approx 26.99\ \text{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Slope continuity at $H_{ip}$:}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{\text{basal}}(H_{ip})
&= c_1 \cdot c_2 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2 - 1} \\
&= -5.2216 \cdot 0.4098 \cdot 3.6^{-0.5902} 
\approx -1.0047
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{\text{upper}}(H_{ip})
&= -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{ip})^{b-1} \\
&= -4.5053 \cdot 0.6292 \cdot 16.4^{-0.3708} 
\approx -1.0047
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

This confirms that the stem profile is $C^1$-continuous and correctly anchored at the measured DBH.


\subsubsection{Analytical Volume Integration}

Using the parameters determined from the analytical stem profile:

\[
H = 20.0\,\text{m}, \quad 
H_{ip} = 3.6\,\text{m}, \quad
d_0 = 35.81\,\text{cm}, \quad
d_{ip} = 27.00\,\text{cm}, \quad
d_t = 0.8\,\text{cm},
\]
\[
a = 4.506, \quad
b = 0.6292, \quad
c_1 = -5.214, \quad
c_2 = 0.4097
\]

we can compute the stem volumes and the merchantable height.

\subsubsection*{Merchantable Height for $d \ge 7$ cm}

The merchantable height $h_7$ is found from the upper segment equation:
\begin{equation}
d(h_7) = a (H - h_7)^b + d_t = 7
\end{equation}
Solving for $h_7$ gives
\begin{equation}
H - h_7 = \left(\frac{7 - d_t}{a}\right)^{1/b}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
h_7 = H - \left(\frac{7 - 0.8}{4.506}\right)^{1/0.6292}
\approx 18.34\ \text{m}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Total Stem Volume}

\textbf{Basal segment volume: $V_{\text{low}}(0, H_{ip})$}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{basal}} &= \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Big[
d_0^2 \cdot H_{ip} 
+ \frac{2 d_0 c_1 H_{ip}^{c_2+1}}{c_2 + 1} 
+ \frac{c_1^2 H_{ip}^{2c_2+1}}{2c_2 + 1} 
\Big] \\
&= \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Big[
35.81^2 \cdot 3.6 
+ \frac{2 \cdot 35.81 \cdot (-5.214) \cdot 3.6^{1.4097}}{1.4097} \\
& + \frac{(-5.214)^2 \cdot 3.6^{2.4097}}{2.4097} 
\Big] \approx 0.248\ \text{m}^3
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Upper segment volume: $V_{\text{up}}(H_{ip}, H)$}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{upper}} &= \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Big[
d_t^2 \cdot (H - H_{ip}) 
- \frac{2 a d_t (H - H_{ip})^{b+1}}{b+1} \\
& - \frac{a^2 (H - H_{ip})^{2b+1}}{2b+1} 
\Big] \\
&= \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \Big[
0.8^2 \cdot 16.4 
- \frac{2 \cdot 4.506 \cdot 0.8 \cdot 16.4^{1.6292}}{1.6292} \\
& - \frac{4.506^2 \cdot 16.4^{2.2584}}{2.2584} 
\Big] \approx 0.425\ \text{m}^3
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\[
\implies V_{\text{total}} = V_{\text{basal}} + V_{\text{upper}} \approx 0.248 + 0.425 = 0.673\ \text{m}^3
\]

\subsubsection*{Merchantable Volume ($V_{d \ge 7}$)}

\subsubsection{Numerical Application of Bark and Under-Bark Volume Integration}

To determine the merchantable wood volume without bark ($V_{\text{wood}}$), the polynomial expansion coefficients $A_i$ are calculated first using the model coefficients $\alpha = 0.085149$, $\beta = 0.060934$, and $\gamma = -0.000228$:
\begin{align*}
    A_0 &= 0.085149^2 \approx 0.00725 \\
    A_1 &= -2 \cdot 0.085149 \cdot (1 - 0.060934) \approx -0.15992 \\
    A_2 &= (1 - 0.060934)^2 + 2 \cdot 0.085149 \cdot (-0.000228) \approx 0.88181 \\
    A_3 &= -2 \cdot (-0.000228) \cdot (1 - 0.060934) \approx 0.00043 \\
    A_4 &= (-0.000228)^2 \approx 5.198 \cdot 10^{-8}
\end{align*}

Using these constants, the antiderivatives $F_{\text{low}}(h)$ and $F_{\text{up}}(z)$ are evaluated at the segment boundaries to compute the definitive wood layers.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Basal Wood Volume Under Bark ($V_{\text{wood, low}}$):}
  The antiderivative $F_{\text{low}}(h)$ accumulates the basal segment polynomial from $h_1 = 0$ to $h_2 = \qty{3.6}{m}$ using $c_0 = 35.814$, $c_1 = -5.2216$, and $c_2 = 0.4098$:
  \begin{equation}
      F_{\text{low}}(h) = \sum_{n=0}^{4} A_n \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} c_0^{n-k} c_1^k \frac{h^{k \cdot c_2 + 1}}{k \cdot c_2 + 1} \right]
  \end{equation}
  Evaluating this expression yields $F_{\text{low}}(0) = 0$ and $F_{\text{low}}(3.6) \approx 2807.55$, resulting in:
  \begin{equation}
      V_{\text{wood, low}} = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \left[ 2807.55 - 0 \right] \approx \qty{0.221}{m^3}
  \end{equation}

  \item \textbf{Upper Wood Volume Under Bark ($V_{\text{wood, up}}$):}
  The antiderivative $F_{\text{up}}(z)$ utilizes the height-transformed boundaries $z_1 = 20 - 3.6 = 16.4$ and $z_2 = 20 - 20 = 0$ with $a = 4.5053$, $b = 0.6292$, and $d_t = 0.8$:
  \begin{equation}
      F_{\text{up}}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{4} A_n \left[ \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} d_t^{n-k} a^k \frac{z^{k \cdot b + 1}}{k \cdot b + 1} \right]
  \end{equation}
  Evaluating this expression yields $F_{\text{up}}(0) = 0$ and $F_{\text{up}}(16.4) \approx 4777.81$. Applying the directional boundary limits results in:
  \begin{equation}
      V_{\text{wood, up}} = \frac{\pi}{40\,000} \left[ 4777.81 - 0 \right] \approx \qty{0.375}{m^3}
  \end{equation}

  \item \textbf{Total Wood and Bark Volumes:}
  Summing both sections determines the total solid wood volume under bark and isolates the exact bark volume via subtraction:
  \begin{equation}
      V_{\text{wood, total}} = 0.221 + 0.375 = \qty{0.596}{m^3}
  \end{equation}
  \begin{equation}
      V_{\text{bark}} = V_{\text{total, ob}} - V_{\text{wood, total}} = 0.673 - 0.596 = \qty{0.077}{m^3}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
This demonstrates that for the sample tree, bark accounts for approximately \qty{11.5}{\%} of the total gross stem volume.


\subsection{Numerical Illustration of Two and Three-Point Calibration}

To demonstrate the multi-diameter calibration routines, the Norway spruce example ($H_{\text{total}} = \qty{20}{m}$, $H_{ip} = \qty{3.6}{m}$, $d_t = \qty{0.8}{cm}$, and regional GLM exponents $b_{\text{glm}} = 0.6292$, $c_{2,\text{glm}} = 0.4098$) is evaluated under different structural measurement scenarios.

\paragraph{Example 1: Two Upper Measurements ($h_1 \geq H_{ip}$ and $h_2 \geq H_{ip}$)}
An upper profile section is captured by measuring $d_1 = \qty{25.0}{cm}$ at $h_1 = \qty{5.0}{m}$ and $d_2 = \qty{20.0}{cm}$ at $h_2 = \qty{10.0}{m}$. Because both measurement heights lie strictly above the inflection point ($H_{ip} = \qty{3.6}{m}$), the shape exponent $b$ and the scale factor $a$ are analytically fixed by the upper canopy geometry:

\begin{equation}
    b = \frac{\ln\left(\frac{20.0 - 0.8}{25.0 - 0.8}\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{20.0 - 10.0}{20.0 - 5.0}\right)} = \frac{-0.2314}{-0.4055} \approx 0.5708
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{25.0 - 0.8}{(20.0 - 5.0)^{0.5708}} = \frac{24.2}{15.0^{0.5708}} \approx 5.1582
\end{equation}

Consequently, the diameter and geometric slope at the inflection point height ($H_{ip} = \qty{3.6}{m}$) are explicitly determined:
\begin{equation}
    d_{ip} = 5.1582 \cdot (20.0 - 3.6)^{0.5708} + 0.8 \approx \qty{26.2645}{cm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_{ip} = -5.1582 \cdot 0.5708 \cdot 16.4^{-0.4292} \approx -0.8863
\end{equation}

Since no breast-height diameter ($\text{DBH}$) was recorded during data collection, the system resolves the missing lower metric via an outer numerical root-finding loop. The algorithm searches for an implicit breast-height diameter ($\text{DBH}_{\text{implicit}}$) that simultaneously satisfies the regional GLM boundary condition for $c_2$ and matches the physical taper equation at $h = \qty{1.3}{m}$:
\begin{equation}
    f(\text{DBH}_{\text{implicit}}) = d(1.3) - \text{DBH}_{\text{implicit}} = 0
\end{equation}

Numerical convergence using the open secant method is achieved at $\text{DBH}_{\text{implicit}} \approx \qty{28.9482}{cm}$. This implicit diameter updates the reference diameter to $d_r = 28.9482 + 1.3 = \qty{30.2482}{cm}$, which in turn calibrates the regional base shape exponent via the GLM logit transformation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{logit}(c_2) &= 7.0974 - 3.1716 \cdot \ln(1 + 20) \\
  &\quad + 0.6313 \cdot \ln(1 + 30.2482) \\
  &= 7.0974 - 9.6560 + 2.1739 = -0.3847 \\
  &\implies c_2 = \frac{e^{-0.3847}}{1 + e^{-0.3847}} \approx 0.4050
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Enforcing $C^1$-continuity at $H_{ip}$ with the newly calibrated $c_2 = 0.4050$ yields the final basal profile coefficients:
\begin{equation}
    c_1 = \frac{s_{ip}}{c_2 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2-1}} = \frac{-0.8863}{0.4050 \cdot 3.6^{-0.5950}} \approx -4.6750
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    c_0 = d_{ip} - c_1 \cdot H_{ip}^{c_2} = 26.2645 - (-4.6750 \cdot 3.6^{0.4050}) \approx 34.1481
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Verification:} Evaluating the lower stem profile at $h = \qty{1.3}{m}$ perfectly reproduces the implicitly solved diameter, confirming mathematical consistency:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d(1.3) &= c_0 + c_1 \cdot 1.3^{c_2} \\
    &= 34.1481 + (-4.6750) \cdot 1.3^{0.4050} \\
    &= 34.1481 - 5.2000 \\
    &= \qty{28.9481}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Example 2: Split Measurements ($h_1 < H_{ip}$ and $h_2 \geq H_{ip}$)}
A typical inventory architecture combines a lower breast-height diameter $d_1 = \qty{30.0}{cm}$ at $h_1 = \qty{1.3}{m}$ with a commercial upper merchantable top-diameter $d_2 = \qty{7.0}{cm}$ at $h_2 = \qty{18.5}{m}$. 

Since the measurements span across the inflection point ($1.3 < 3.6 \leq 18.5$), the system forms an implicit trajectory problem. The value of $b$ must satisfy the root-finding criteria where the lower polynomial boundary condition perfectly meets $d_1$ at $h_1$:
\begin{equation}
    f(b) = \big(c_0(b) + c_1(b) \cdot h_1^{c_{2,\text{glm}}}\big) - d_1 = 0
\end{equation}

For a given trial value of $b$, the dependent trajectory parameters are sequentially structured from the upper measurement equation:
\begin{equation}
    a(b) = \frac{7.0 - 0.8}{(20.0 - 18.5)^b} = \frac{6.2}{1.5^b}
\end{equation}
Evaluating the root-finding function via bisection inside the code over the unit interval $[0.01, 1.0]$ numerical convergence is achieved at $b \approx 0.6041$. The step-by-step resolution of the profile sequence follows:
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{6.2}{1.5^{0.6041}} \approx 4.8531
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    d_{ip} = 4.8531 \cdot (20.0 - 3.6)^{0.6041} + 0.8 \approx \qty{27.0963}{cm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    s_{ip} = -4.8531 \cdot 0.6041 \cdot 16.4^{-0.3959} \approx -0.9686
\end{equation}

Using $c_{2,\text{glm}} = 0.4098$, the lower base coefficients resolve to:
\begin{equation}
    c_1 = \frac{-0.9686}{0.4098 \cdot 3.6^{-0.5902}} \approx -5.0340
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    c_0 = 27.0963 - (-5.0340 \cdot 3.6^{0.4098}) \approx 35.6054
\end{equation}

\noindent verification of the lower boundary anchor checks out seamlessly:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d(1.3) &= 35.6054 + (-5.0340) \cdot 1.3^{0.4098} \\
    &= 35.6054 - 5.6054 \\
    &= \qty{30.0}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Example 3: Full Profile Calibration via Three Measurements ($n_{\text{pts}} \geq 3$)}
The adaptive profile integration is demonstrated using three observed data points distributed along the stem: a basal diameter $d_1 = \qty{30.0}{cm}$ at stump level $h_1 = \qty{0.5}{m}$, a mid-shaft diameter $d_2 = \qty{24.0}{cm}$ at $h_2 = \qty{6.0}{m}$, and an upper canopy diameter $d_3 = \qty{15.0}{cm}$ at $h_3 = \qty{14.0}{m}$.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Canopy Shape Exponent ($b$):} Since the upper two measurements ($h_2, h_3$) lie strictly above the inflection point height ($H_{ip} \approx \qty{3.5898}{m}$), the main-shaft form exponent $b$ is calibrated analytically using the log-ratio of the remaining tree lengths to the terminal tip ($H = \qty{20.0}{m}$):
  \begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    b &= \frac{\ln\left(\frac{15.0 - 0.8}{24.0 - 0.8}\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{20.0 - 14.0}{20.0 - 6.0}\right)} = \frac{\ln(14.2) - \ln(23.2)}{\ln(6.0) - \ln(14.0)} \\
    &= \frac{-0.4909}{-0.8473} \approx 0.5794
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Upper Scale Parameter ($a$):} Anchoring the upper profile trajectory to the mid-diameter measurement at $h_2 = \qty{6.0}{m}$ isolates the scaling factor $a$:
    \begin{equation}
        a = \frac{d_2 - d_t}{(H - h_2)^b} = \frac{24.0 - 0.8}{(20.0 - 6.0)^{0.5794}} = \frac{23.2}{14.0^{0.5794}} \approx 5.0285
    \end{equation}

  \item \textbf{Inflection Point State Variables ($d_{ip}, s_{ip}$):} Transitioning down to the mathematically derived inflection height ($H_{ip} = \qty{3.5898}{m}$) evaluates the local diameter and geometric slope:
    \begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d_{ip} &= a \cdot (H - H_{ip})^b + d_t \\
    &= 5.0285 \cdot (20.0 - 3.5898)^{0.5794} + 0.8 \\
    &\approx \qty{26.2365}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    s_{ip} &= -a \cdot b \cdot (H - H_{ip})^{b-1} \\
    &= -5.0285 \cdot 0.5794 \cdot 16.4102^{-0.4206} \\
    &\approx -0.8981
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Basal Polynomial Optimization ($c_0, c_1, c_2$):} The remaining basal measurement $d_1 = \qty{30.0}{cm}$ at $h_1 = \qty{0.5}{m}$ is used to solve the lower stem profile. Enforcing $C^1$-continuity at $H_{ip}$ defines the shape parameter functions:
    \begin{equation}
        c_2(c_0) = \frac{s_{ip} \cdot H_{ip}}{d_{ip} - c_0}, \quad \text{and} \quad c_1(c_0) = \frac{d_{ip} - c_0}{H_{ip}^{c_2(c_0)}}
    \end{equation}
    
    The code executes the inner optimization to find the parameter intercept $c_0$ that minimizes the squared residual error at the stump measurement: $\min_{c_0} (c_0 + c_1 \cdot h_1^{c_2} - d_1)^2$. Because the input data represents a typical biological stem geometry, the parameter converges freely inside the valid domain without hitting the upper boundary restrictions:
    \begin{equation}
        c_0 \approx 31.7226, \quad c_2 \approx 0.5876, \quad \text{and} \quad c_1 \approx -2.5887
    \end{equation}

    \noindent \textbf{Boundary and Implicit Anchor Verification:} Evaluating the completed basal profile at stump height $h_1 = \qty{0.5}{m}$ and at standard breast height $h = \qty{1.3}{m}$ confirms seamless mathematical integrity and model convergence:
    \begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d(0.5) &= 31.7226 + (-2.5887) \cdot 0.5^{0.5876} \\
    &\approx \qty{30.0}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    d(1.3) &= 31.7226 + (-2.5887) \cdot 1.3^{0.5876} \\
    &\approx \qty{28.7025}{cm}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
   
    The outer root-finding loop thus converges cleanly at an implicit breast-height diameter of $\text{DBH}_{\text{implicit}} \approx \qty{28.7025}{cm}$.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Discussion}

The composite taper model developed in this study provides a biologically interpretable and mathematically consistent framework for describing the stem form of Norway spruce across a wide range of tree dimensions. By explicitly separating the stem into a lower neiloidic section and an upper parabolic section connected at a dynamically estimated inflection point ($H_{\text{ip}}$), the model captures the principal geometric and morphologic transitions that characterise stems while maintaining analytical tractability and ensuring continuous diameter and slope transitions ($C^1$ continuity).

The results demonstrate that a high parameter efficiency---utilising only eight core parameters ($a, b, c_1, c_2, d_0, d_{\text{ip}}, H_{\text{ip}}, d_t$)---is sufficient to describe both local stem diameters and total stem volume with high precision, completely avoiding breaks, jumps or unphysical oscillations. In particular, the introduction of a biologically constrained inflection point enables the model to accommodate the gradual transition from the root flare to the upper stem without requiring high-order polynomials or spline formulations that may produce unrealistic behaviour between measurement points.

While the parameter determination requires a brief numerical iteration to anchor the model at measured diameters, the subsequent application of the model---such as calculating volumes or diameters---is entirely analytical, eliminating the need for numerical integration in operational use. The approach treats the DBH as a strict geometric constraint, ensuring that legacy inventory data is fully preserved in the resulting stem profile.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations and Operational Challenges}

Several limitations regarding the empirical database and field application should be acknowledged. First, the dataset contains comparatively few very small trees. Although the model formulation remains mathematically valid for juvenile individuals, additional observations from early developmental stages would improve confidence regarding the behaviour of the inflection-point model and the associated shape exponents near the lower boundary of the observed height range. 

To establish a robust lower anchor under these current data limitations, the diameter at ground level ($d_0$) had to be estimated using an iterative smoothing approach based on all available measurements up to a height of 5\,m. While restricting this estimation exclusively to measurements below the inflection point would be theoretically superior, it was impractical due to the insufficient number of diameter observations per tree in smaller dimension classes. Future data collection should prioritise measurements as close to $d_0$ as operationally feasible.

The definition of ground level itself introduces operational uncertainty, particularly on steep alpine terrain where the transition between stem and root system is often morphologically ambiguous. Standardised measurement protocols for basal diameters are therefore necessary before broader application of similar taper formulations, as variations in this baseline can significantly alter both the captured root flare geometry and the subsequent volume predictions.

The remaining systematic residual trends observed at $0.3H_{\text{total}}$ and $0.65H_{\text{total}}$ suggest that further refinement of the taper formulation is possible. Future research should target alternative approaches, including additional stem segments, variable transition functions, or the development of dedicated parameterisations optimized specifically for multi-point sampling designs to eliminate the structural legacy of the baseline population expectations.

\subsection{Biological Interpretation of Stem Shape Dynamics}

The estimated inflection-point model suggests that the absolute height of the root flare increases with tree size, but at a diminishing rate. Consequently, the relative position of the inflection point shifts downward within the stem profile as trees become taller. This behaviour is consistent with the mechanical role of the lower stem, where increasing tree height requires additional basal reinforcement while the overall stem length increases more rapidly than the vertical extent of the root flare.

The estimated shape exponents further support this interpretation. The upper-stem exponent $b$ increased with tree dimensions, indicating a gradual transition from relatively conical juvenile stems towards fuller and more parabolic stem profiles in mature individuals.

The basal exponent $c_2$ exhibited the opposite trend. Increasing tree height was associated with lower values of $c_2$, corresponding to a stronger neiloidic expansion near the stem base. This finding is biologically plausible, as larger trees require greater structural support to withstand increasing wind loads and bending moments. The positive influence of reference diameter partially counteracted this trend, suggesting that trees of similar height but greater diameter tend to exhibit a somewhat less pronounced basal flare. Together, these relationships indicate that both stem sections respond differently to increasing tree size: whereas the upper stem gradually approaches a fuller profile, the basal stem increasingly reflects biomechanical requirements associated with stability and load transfer.

\subsection{Performance of Diameter Predictions and Optimization Trade-offs}

The diameter validations revealed that the model reproduces stem diameters with good overall precision across the observed range of tree sizes. Nevertheless, systematic trends were detected at both validation heights. At $0.3H$, the model tended to underestimate smaller diameters and overestimate larger diameters, whereas the opposite pattern was observed at $0.65H$. 

The inversion of these trends reflects a structural limitation of the parsimonious two-segment formulation, but is more deeply linked to the chosen optimization strategy and its specific weighting scheme. To capture the biological profile, the model coefficients were estimated using a weighted objective function where the primary priority was to eliminate any bias in the total cumulative volume, while simultaneously maximising the fit of both sub-volumes ($V_{\text{low}}$ and $V_{\text{up}}$). Because of the parsimonious mathematical structure, a flawless fit for all local diameters and sub-volumes simultaneously is mathematically constrained. Therefore, the minor localized diameter distortions at $0.3H$ and $0.65H$ represent an accepted trade-off; these localized errors effectively compensate for each other during integration, satisfying the strict constraint of overall volume unbiasedness while maintaining a balanced population fit. 

Real stems may contain additional localised transitions varying among individuals, suggesting that introducing more than two segments could further reduce local bias. However, since these differences remain small, the current model provides a highly usable and robust compromise. Furthermore, residual analyses showed no evidence of increasing variance across the observed ranges of tree height or reference diameter, indicating that the selected transformations and logit link functions successfully captured the principal non-linear relationships governing stem form development.

\subsection{Volume Estimation, Geometric Leverage, and Multi-Point Calibration}

The volume analyses illustrate the trade-off between population-level generalisation and tree-specific calibration, visually demonstrated by the varying envelope spaces in the parameter configurations (Figure~\ref{fig:nMesspunkte}).

Using only total height and a single reference diameter, the model produced unbiased volume estimates across the investigated dimensional range, as the GLM-derived shape exponents adequately represent the average stem form of the underlying Norway spruce population. Under this baseline approach, the mathematical framework exhibits maximum phenotypic flexibility: while the anchoring routine forces the profile strictly through the fixed coordinates at breast height, the upper and basal shape exponents ($b$ and $c_2$) vary across their regional biological boundaries. Relying solely on regional GLM predictions can therefore introduce localized volume errors if the specific tree deviates from the population average.

This morphological uncertainty is drastically constrained upon the inclusion of a second diameter measurement ($d_2$), compressing the remaining volumetric variance into narrow mathematical boundaries. Forcing the composite profile through two independent empirical coordinates sharply seals the directional envelope of the shaft.

However, the exact geometric impact depends heavily on the vertical placement of this second measurement. When positioned lower on the stem at 30\% height ($d_{0.3\text{H}}$), forcing the model through an empirical coordinate while inheriting the unadjusted, frozen baseline shape parameters overstrains the basal root flare adjustment. The geometric tension required to force intersection at $h_2 = 0.3H_{\text{total}}$ is absorbed entirely by the scaling parameter $a$, explaining the non-linear U-shaped residual bias observed in the integrated volume validation (Figure~\ref{fig:residualsVol2}). 

Conversely, relocating this second measurement higher up to 65\% height ($d_{0.65\text{H}}$) shifts this geometric tension further up the shaft. Because the inherited regional shape parameters are not specifically optimized for this multi-point constraint, the mathematical routine accommodates the high-altitude coordinate by shifting the profile trajectory, which successfully minimizes overall variance but introduces a different trade-off. This rigid constraint forces a slight over-flattening of the profile in large trees, which directly manifests as a systematic monotonic overestimation trend at the upper dimensional margins of the volume estimates (Figure~\ref{fig:residualsVol2b}).

Importantly, these systematic differences observed between $d_{0.3H}$ and $d_{0.65H}$ directly propagate into distinct volume discrepancies depending on the selected calibration height. These geometric leveraging effects and residual patterns are inherently tied to the chosen mathematical formulation of this model and may not necessarily apply to alternative taper functions or algebraic structures.

Regarding the persistent residual bias observed across the volume spectrum in both two-diameter calibration routines, a critical biometric nuance must be clarified. This systematic distortion does not represent an inherent flaw of the split-segment architecture itself, but rather a structural legacy of its parameterization: the underlying regional GLM predictions for the upper shaft exponent $b$ and the basal exponent $c_2$ were originally fitted and optimized exclusively for the single-diameter baseline configuration. Freezing these exponents to baseline population expectations deprives the two-diameter routines of the specific mathematical alignment needed to smoothly integrate a second empirical coordinate into the segmented framework. 

Crucially, this diagnostic suggests that the observed volumetric bias could be systematically eliminated by refitting and calibrating separate, dedicated GLM equations for $b$ and $c_2$. It must be emphasized that no single, generalized two-diameter GLM parameterization exists; instead, separate equations must be tailored specifically to the exact height-combination layout utilized, distinguishing strictly between a $\text{DBH}+d_{0.3H}$ configuration and a $\text{DBH}+d_{0.65H}$ configuration to account for the fundamentally distinct geometric leverage at different relative sampling heights. However, a vital mathematical limitation must be noted: certain sampling configurations may remain fundamentally prone to persistent bias despite customized GLM tuning. If the two empirical coordinates are positioned in too close vertical proximity, such as a clustered lower stem setup, the capacity of the exponents to modulate the transition through $H_{ip}$ is structurally restricted, and the system suffers from a critical lack of geometric leverage. In such poorly spaced layouts, the mathematical elasticity of the exponents is insufficient to map the unmeasured stem sections accurately, underscoring that a bias-free two-diameter calibration strictly requires a minimal, strategic vertical separation between the anchors.

The deterministic three-point calibration architecture achieves absolute geometric closure. By extracting all critical shape parameters ($a, b, c_0, c_1, c_2$) sequentially and tree-specifically from three empirical coordinates, the mathematical flexibility of the composite power function collapses to zero. There exists only one unique, mathematically continuous, and $C^1$-smooth profile line that can simultaneously intersect the root collar swell ($d_1$), the intermediate shaft thickness ($d_{0.3H}$), and the merchantable top boundary ($d_{0.65H}$). The shrinkage of the profile envelope down to a single deterministic line accounts for the dramatic halving of the residual standard deviation ($\sigma = 0.0279$) and the elimination of localized volume trends (Figure~\ref{fig:residualsVol3}). 

While this progression shifts the biometric framework from a regional, stochastic approximation to a true, tree-specific analytical volume derivation, providing maximum inventory precision under ideal conditions, this geometric rigidity introduces a profound vulnerability under realistic field noise conditions.

\subsection{Measurement Error Propagation and Volumetric Bias}

While increasing the sampling intensity structurally constrains the shape envelope, it simultaneously introduces a critical trade-off regarding error propagation. Squeezing the parameter space down to a zero-variance deterministic line exposes the calibration pipeline to severe parameter distortion and mathematical over-constraints when confronted with real-world field measurement noise. To systematically isolate these geometric mechanics, separate empirical noise scenarios are evaluated against a reference individual ($H_{\text{total}} = \qty{25}{m}$, base $\text{DBH} = \qty{25}{cm}$).

Due to the quadratic nature of diameter within the cross-sectional area integration ($V \propto \int d(h)^2 \, dh$), the propagation of these identical diameter shifts is inherently asymmetrical. The volume inflation caused by positive measurement errors systematically deviates from the volume reduction of equivalent negative errors. In large-scale forest inventories where diameter field measurements are subject to symmetrical noise, these multi-point geometric distortions do not cancel out, introducing a systematic macro-level overestimation bias.

\subsubsection{Implications for Forest Inventory Applications and Design Guidelines}

Ultimately, this multidimensional sensitivity analysis evaluates isolated and compounded diameter noise under a static, error-free height framework. In operational forest inventories, total tree height ($H_{\text{total}}$) represents an additional, frequently highly volatile source of non-systematic measurement error. Within the $C^1$-continuous composite taper function used here, any distortion in the recorded height directly shifts the relative vertical positions of the intermediate ($0.3H_{\text{total}}$) and upper ($0.65H_{\text{total}}$) sampling heights. More critically, it alters the mathematical evaluation of the upper parabolic stem segment $a(H_{\text{total}} - h)^b + d_t$ and simultaneously displaces the non-linear inflection boundary ($H_{\text{ip}}$) where the function transitions to the basal power system $c_0 + c_1 h^{c_2}$. This height-induced spatial displacement introduces a secondary layer of complex parameter leverage that interacts non-linearly with the diameter noise, further exacerbating the operational risk of over-constraining the calibration pipeline.

Regarding the persistent residual bias observed across the volume spectrum in both two-diameter calibration routines (Figure~\ref{fig:residualsVol2} and~\ref{fig:residualsVol2b}), a critical biometric nuance must be clarified. This systematic distortion does not represent an inherent flaw of the split-segment architecture itself, but rather a structural legacy of its parameterisation: the underlying regional GLM predictions for the upper shaft exponent $b$ and the basal exponent $c_2$ were originally fitted and optimised exclusively for the single-diameter baseline configuration. Freezing these exponents to baseline population expectations deprives the two-diameter routines of the specific mathematical alignment needed to smoothly integrate a second empirical coordinate into the segmented framework. 

Crucially, this diagnostic suggests that the observed volumetric bias could be systematically eliminated by refitting and calibrating separate, dedicated GLM equations for the shape parameters $b$ and $c_2$. No single, generalised two-diameter GLM parameterisation exists; instead, separate equations must be tailored specifically to the exact height-combination layout utilised, distinguishing strictly between a $\text{DBH}+d_{0.3\text{H}}$ configuration and a $\text{DBH}+d_{0.65\text{H}}$ configuration to account for the fundamentally distinct geometric leverage at different relative sampling heights. This necessity arises because the empirical stem curves exhibit slight deviations from a perfectly smooth geometric form; if the taper function perfectly matched the real stem profile at all points, a single parameterisation would suffice to reproduce volumes accurately across different height combinations.

However, certain sampling configurations may remain fundamentally prone to persistent bias despite customised GLM tuning. If the two empirical coordinates are positioned in too close vertical proximity, such as a clustered lower-stem setup, the capacity of the exponents to modulate the transition through $H_{\text{ip}}$ is structurally restricted, and the system suffers from a critical lack of geometric leverage. In such poorly spaced layouts, the mathematical elasticity of the exponents is insufficient to map the unmeasured stem sections accurately, underscoring that a bias-free two-diameter calibration strictly requires a minimal, strategic vertical separation between the anchors.

It is worth noting that while this validation focuses primarily on integrated volumetric outputs, the mathematical design of the $C^1$-continuous composite power function inherently guarantees highly localised diameter precision along the entire stem profile. Because the deterministic multi-point frameworks enforce exact interpolation at their empirical coordinates while minimising localised shape curvature, the low integrated volume variances act as a geometric proxy confirming the absence of internal diameter oscillations or cumulative taper drift. Unlike traditional polynomial splines, which frequently suffer from unphysiological mathematical swinging between structural anchors, the strict parameter boundaries of this parabolic and power function framework successfully suppress localised diameter deviations without requiring dense section-by-section empirical measurements. 

From a practical inventory perspective, field measurements are inherently subject to stochastic, symmetrical noise where individual diameter deviations follow a zero-mean distribution. For the single-diameter baseline configuration, which scales near-linearly with diameter inflation, these random errors largely cancel out in the aggregate, introducing negligible systematic drift at the population level. Under such circumstances, robustness and stable error propagation are often more important than maximum theoretical precision. 

Conversely, because the multi-point frameworks introduce severe, highly non-linear parameter leverage, this error symmetry breaks down. Whether the random sampling errors happen to manifest as co-directional or counter-directional shifts in a given tree, the asymmetric volumetric response under extreme non-linear distortions ensures that positive and negative errors do not compensate for each other. Consequently, propagating purely random field noise through these rigid architectures might translates into a systematic macro-level overestimation bias when aggregating data across entire forest stands. It must be explicitly constrained, however, that this severe non-linear amplification is a distinct characteristic of the specific generalised power and parabolic function framework characterised here. It does not automatically apply to all multi-point biometric methods; alternative mathematical approaches, such as unconstrained smoothing splines or generalised additive models, may possess fundamentally different error propagation profiles and could potentially maintain near-linear error symmetries under identical sampling noise.

Therefore, to safeguard inventory resilience against stochastically induced drift in large-scale operational applications, the robust single-diameter baseline configuration or the strategically cushioned two-diameter variant utilising $d_{0.65\text{H}}$ remain the most viable choices. Where additional stem measurements can be obtained reliably, the inclusion of an upper-stem diameter via the $d_{0.65\text{H}}$ approach appears particularly promising because it achieves a considerable reduction in model uncertainty while maintaining relatively stable, error-dampening behaviour under measurement noise. 

The rigid three-diameter framework, by contrast, is best viewed as a tree-specific reconstruction approach rather than a conventional inventory model. Such a methodology lacks the mathematical elasticity to absorb field-level data conflicts and should be strictly reserved for scenarios where upper-stem diameters are obtained using terrestrial laser scanning (TLS), photogrammetry, or other high-precision measurement systems, where rigorous instrumental calibration and strict quality assurance can completely suppress operational scattering.


\subsection{Relationship to Classical Form Factors}

A significant advantage of the proposed model is its ability to analytically derive the reference diameter form factor ($f_{r} = V_{\text{total}} / (A_{r} \cdot H_{\text{total}}$)), dynamically adjusting to individual stem morphology. 

The form factor can be related to any arbitrary reference diameter $d_r$. When utilising $d_r$, the formulation ensures mathematical stability and prevents the form factor from approaching infinity for small trees ($H_{\text{total}} < \qty{1.3}{m}$), where the classical breast height diameter (DBH) is either undefined or approaches zero.

The corresponding reference basal area $A_r$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
A_r = \frac{\pi}{4} d_r^2
\end{equation}

The total volume of the tree $V_{\text{total}}$ is obtained by integrating the lower and upper stem segments using the inflection point height $H_{\text{ip}}$ as the structural boundary:
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{total}} = \frac{\pi}{4} \left( \hat{V}_{low} + \hat{V}_{up} \right)
\end{equation}

Finally, the dynamic reference form factor $f_r$ is derived by dividing $V_{\text{total}}$ by the volume of the reference cylinder ($V_B = A_r \cdot H_{\text{total}}$). Since the factor $\frac{\pi}{4}$ cancels out, the analytical expression simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
f_r = \frac{\hat{V}_{low} + \hat{V}_{up}}{d_r^2 \cdot H_{\text{total}}}
\end{equation}

where $\hat{V}_{low}$ and $\hat{V}_{up}$ represent the analytically integrated components from the segmented taper equations:
\begin{align}
\hat{V}_{low} &= c_0^2 H_{\text{ip}} + \frac{2c_0 c_1 H_{\text{ip}}^{c_2+1}}{c_2+1} + \frac{c_1^2 H_{\text{ip}}^{2c_2+1}}{2c_2+1} \\
\hat{V}_{up} &= d_t^2 (H_{\text{total}} - H_{\text{ip}}) + \frac{2 a d_t (H_{\text{total}}-H_{\text{ip}})^{b+1}}{b+1} \nonumber \\
& \quad + \frac{a^2 (H_{\text{total}}-H_{\text{ip}})^{2b+1}}{2b+1}
\end{align}


\section{Conclusion}

This study presents a $C^1$-continuous, biologically constrained, and analytically tractable stem taper model for Norway spruce (\textit{Picea abies}) that unifies traditional forest biometrics with modern high-resolution inventory approaches. By combining a neiloidic basal section with a parabolic upper stem connected through a dynamically estimated inflection point, the framework provides accurate predictions of stem diameters and total volume while maintaining strict geometric continuity and biologically realistic taper behaviour.

The key advantages of the model can be summarised as follows:

\begin{description}
\item[Analytical Precision and Efficiency:] The formulation provides closed-form solutions for stem diameter, height inversion, and volume calculation, eliminating the need for computationally intensive integration and approximation methods, thereby ensuring maximum efficiency in large-scale inventory applications.
\item[Morphological and Ontogenetic Fidelity:] The dynamically estimated inflection point ($H_{\text{ip}}$) captures systematic changes in stem architecture with tree development, accurately mapping the transition from basal root flare to the increasingly parabolic upper stem, and avoiding unphysiological oscillations in stem form.
\item[Applicability Across Tree Sizes:] The model relies on a flexible reference diameter rather than a fixed breast-height measurement, maintaining mathematical stability from saplings to mature trees and enabling consistent application across all ontogenetic stages.
\item[Sensitivity to Measurement Design:] Calibration intensity and sampling configuration strongly influence model performance. Single-diameter calibrations provide robust, unbiased volume estimates suitable for operational inventories, while strategically placed upper-stem measurements---particularly around $0.65H_{\text{total}}$---improve tree-specific precision without excessively amplifying stochastic noise. Rigid three-diameter frameworks offer highest theoretical accuracy but require high-precision measurements and are most appropriate for individual tree reconstructions.
\item[Error Propagation and Operational Guidelines:] Multi-point calibration introduces non-linear parameter leverage, such that random measurement noise no longer cancels out in the aggregate. Consequently, configurations that are optimal under ideal, noise-free conditions may be less robust under realistic field constraints. These findings inform best practices for sampling design and inventory strategies.
\end{description}

Overall, the proposed framework provides a versatile and computationally efficient tool for stem reconstruction, volume estimation, and growth analysis. Its analytical formulation, biological interpretability, and flexibility in measurement configuration make it suitable for both large-scale forest inventories and tree-specific applications.
